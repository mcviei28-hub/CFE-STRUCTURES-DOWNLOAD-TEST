SHEET 01 / STRUCTURES

Chassis

The structure that locates every other subsystem, protects the driver, and sets the baseline stiffness everything else is tuned against.
CONSTRUCTIONTube frame / Monocoque
GOVERNING RULESFSAE Structure Articles
PRIMARY OUTPUTFrame CAD + SES
KEY REVIEWStructural Equivalency
DESIGN PROCESS — CHASSIS
01

Set design targets and read the rulebook first

Before any CAD, pull the current FSAE structural requirements — driver free body template, minimum tube sizes for a baseline space frame, main hoop / front hoop envelope, and impact zone requirements. These aren't suggestions — they're the boundary conditions every later decision has to satisfy, and tech inspection will check them literally.

02

Packaging study

Build a rough envelope around the driver template, powertrain, battery/accumulator (EV) or engine (IC), and provisional suspension hardpoints. This is where wheelbase, track, seating position, and roll hoop locations get fixed. Everything downstream — suspension geometry, pedal box, wiring runs — inherits these decisions, so packaging changes get expensive fast once other subteams start designing around them.

03

Choose construction method

Steel space frame is the default baseline — it's the configuration the rules are written around, and meeting it requires no extra paperwork. A monocoque or hybrid frame (composite tub with a tube subframe) can save mass and add stiffness, but requires an SES submission proving equivalent strength. For a team with a carryover chassis, this decision may already be made — your job shifts to validating or incrementally improving what exists.

04

Structural Equivalency (if not baseline steel)

Any deviation from the baseline steel tube spec — different material, different wall thickness, composite panels — has to be proven equivalent or superior via the SES. This means real hand calculations and/or FEA comparing your design's bending stiffness, buckling strength, and energy absorption against the rulebook's baseline case, submitted and accepted before the car can compete. Treat the SES deadline as a hard gate on your schedule, not a formality — it drives your material and joining decisions upstream.

05

Load cases and analysis

Derive the load cases the frame has to survive: torsional stiffness under diagonal wheel lift, front/side/rear impact per the rulebook's impact requirements, and point loads at harness and hoop attachment points. Run hand calcs for a sanity check, then FEA for the full picture, and always report a factor of safety against yield and against buckling — not just one.

06

Node and joint design

Frame strength lives in the joints, not the tubes. Every node where three or more tubes meet needs a plan for notching, weld access, and gusseting where loads triangulate poorly. This is where a lot of first-year designs quietly lose the stiffness their hand calcs promised — clean triangulation beats a slightly thicker tube almost every time.

07

Build jig, fabricate, verify

A frame is only as accurate as the fixture it's welded on. Build (or inherit and check) a jig that holds every hardpoint to CAD tolerance, tack-weld in sequence to control heat distortion, and measure the finished frame against CAD before anything gets bolted to it permanently. Document weld procedures and get an experienced welder or advisor to inspect critical joints — main hoop and front bulkhead welds especially.

FOR A CARRYOVER CHASSIS Your process compresses to steps 04–06: audit the existing SES against this year's rules for changes, re-verify load cases if anything mounted to the frame has changed mass or location, and inspect real joints for fatigue or damage before you inherit someone else's structure as your baseline.

TORSIONAL RIGIDITY TARGET≥ 2,000 ft·lb / degreeThe frame should be noticeably stiffer than the suspension bolted to it, or your carefully tuned kinematics won't actually reach the tire.
VERTICAL CGBelow frame centerlineRequired to pass the FSAE 60° tilt test — check this early, before battery and seat height decisions are locked in.
HOW WE MEASURE CENTER OF GRAVITY Zero everything on a level floor first. With the rear wheels on a scale and the front lifted to a known angle, the moment reading in in·lb divided by the car's total weight gives the horizontal distance from your zero point — that line sets your CG. Level the car and repeat from the side using the rear wheel as your pivot point, then use trig on that same setup to find vertical CG.
LOOKING AHEAD Formal load-capacity and material-stiffness analysis — knowing exactly what each brace can carry and where it needs reinforcement — usually isn't a first-year priority, but it's core chassis knowledge worth building now: get comfortable thinking through how vertical, horizontal, and diagonal loads actually move through a frame before you're the one deciding where a gusset goes.
ITEM
TERM
DEFINITION
C.01
SPACE FRAME
A structure built from tubes arranged in triangulated patterns, carrying loads primarily as tension/compression along each tube rather than bending. The FSAE baseline construction method — steel tube of specified minimum diameter and wall thickness.
C.02
MONOCOQUE
A "single-shell" structure where the skin itself carries structural loads, typically composite sandwich panels (carbon fiber skins over a foam or honeycomb core) rather than a tube skeleton. Lower mass potential, higher fabrication complexity and cost, and always requires an SES.
C.03
SES (Structural Equivalency Spreadsheet)
The required submission proving that any non-baseline chassis material or construction meets or exceeds the strength, stiffness, and energy-absorption properties of the rulebook's default steel space frame. Reviewed by FSAE technical staff before the car may compete — miss the deadline and the car doesn't run regardless of how well it's built.
C.04
MAIN HOOP
The rollover hoop located near the driver's shoulders, one of the two primary structures (with the front hoop) that must clear the driver's helmet in every direction per the rulebook's roll hoop envelope requirements.
C.05
FRONT HOOP
The rollover hoop located above the driver's legs, near the steering wheel. Works with the main hoop and its bracing to define the survival cell around the driver.
C.06
NODE
A junction point where multiple frame tubes meet. Node design — angle, gusseting, weld access — determines whether a frame achieves the stiffness its tube-level hand calcs predict.
C.07
GUSSET
A local reinforcing plate or tube segment added at a joint to carry load that would otherwise concentrate as bending at a poorly-triangulated node.
C.08
FREE BODY (DIAGRAM)
A simplified sketch isolating a component or structure with every external force and reaction shown, used as the first step of any hand calculation. Also refers to the rulebook's driver template used to verify cockpit and hoop clearance.
C.09
FEA (Finite Element Analysis)
Software-based structural analysis that divides a part into small elements to approximate stress, strain, and deflection under load. Powerful, but only as trustworthy as its boundary conditions and mesh — always sanity-check against a hand calc.
C.10
FACTOR OF SAFETY (FoS)
The ratio of a material's failure strength to the actual applied stress. A FoS of 2 means the part can theoretically take twice its expected load before failing. Report FoS against both yield and buckling — they're rarely the same number.
C.11
TUBE NOTCHING (COPING)
Cutting the end of a tube to match the outer profile of the tube it's joining, maximizing weld contact area and joint strength at an intersection.
C.12
TIG WELDING
Tungsten Inert Gas welding — the standard joining method for FSAE steel tube frames, chosen for precision and weld quality on thin-wall tubing.
C.13
TORSIONAL STIFFNESS
A frame's resistance to twisting along its longitudinal axis, usually reported in N·m per degree. It's the chassis property suspension tuning is built on top of — a frame that twists too easily undermines even well-designed suspension kinematics.
SHEET 02 / STRUCTURES

Suspension

Controls how the tire contact patch moves relative to the chassis — the single biggest lever on how the car actually drives.
ARCHITECTUREDouble wishbone
TOOLSKinematics software + FEA
PRIMARY OUTPUTHardpoints + A-arms
KEY REVIEWKinematics validation
DESIGN PROCESS — SUSPENSION
01

Define vehicle dynamics targets

Start from what the car needs to do, not what looks good in CAD: target weight distribution, front/rear roll stiffness split, wheelbase, and track width. These come from a conversation with vehicle dynamics/driver feedback, not from suspension alone — chassis packaging and tire selection both constrain what's actually achievable. As a starting point, run as short a wheelbase as your packaging allows (FSAE's floor is 60 in) with track around 75–80% of wheelbase.

02

Choose architecture

Double wishbone is the near-universal FSAE choice — it gives independent control over camber and roll center behavior that a strut or trailing-arm setup can't match at this scale. The real design decisions are in geometry, not architecture.

03

Kinematics in software

Model the linkage in kinematics software before touching structural CAD. You're solving for camber gain through bump and roll, minimal bump steer, sensible roll center height and migration, and scrub radius. Static camber is usually set from the tire manufacturer's optimal spec plus 1–1.5° of adjustment range to tune with. A longer lower A-arm makes rising camber easier to dial in — as a starting ratio, keep the upper arm roughly 50–80% of the lower arm's length. This step gets iterated dozens of times cheaply in software — it's the expensive mistake to make in metal instead.

04

Hardpoint packaging

Check every hardpoint against the chassis envelope, brake caliper and rotor clearance, wheel and rim offset, and steering rack/tie rod path. This is the step that most often sends suspension back to step 03 — a kinematically ideal point that collides with the upright or brake package isn't usable.

05

Spring, damper, and motion ratio

Size motion ratio for your rocker/bellcrank, then select spring rate and damper to hit your target ride frequency and roll stiffness split — usually landing in a range validated by past team data or lap simulation rather than derived from first principles alone. A reasonable starting linkage ratio is 2 in of spring travel per 1 in of wheel travel. Decide linear vs. progressive springs here too, and mind spring orientation — the smaller coil end should seat against the retainer.

06

Load cases and component design

Derive worst-case loads — hard cornering, hard braking, pothole/curb strike — and design A-arms and uprights against them. Use rod ends at A-arm-to-upright and A-arm-to-chassis joints so the linkage behaves as the pin-jointed model your kinematics software assumed.

07

Manufacture, assemble, and set up

Build to the jig or fixture, then verify measured kinematics against the model before the car turns a wheel in anger. Finish with corner weighting and alignment — a perfectly designed suspension with sloppy static setup will still handle poorly.

WHY WE RUN NEGATIVE CAMBER Start the car at neutral camber with no driver aboard, and the suspension settles into slight negative camber once the driver's weight is on it — that's intentional, not a compromise. It looks like it costs you contact patch on a straight, but the course is full of corners: negative camber is what keeps the tire flat on the road under the lateral load of cornering, when it matters most.
SPRINGS & DAMPERS, PRACTICALLY A damper controls oil flow through a piston full of small holes — bigger holes, faster flow, less resistance. Remote reservoirs keep the damping fluid from overheating and usually add a knob to adjust flow. Bench-test spring choices under different loads, then set damping until oscillation is controlled without feeling dead. The target is always a balance between pitch control and mechanical traction — chase one too hard and you lose the other.
READING YOUR CONTACT PATCH Cheapest test in the shop: roll the car over a strip of chalky concrete with a driver aboard, and turn it through some steering angle. The chalk transfer shows you exactly how camber, caster, and toe are actually shaping the contact patch — a fast, low-cost way to build intuition before you touch a setup sheet.
ITEM
TERM
DEFINITION
S.01
DOUBLE WISHBONE
A suspension architecture using two A-shaped control arms (upper and lower) per corner, giving independent control of camber curve and roll center location — the standard for FSAE.
S.02
A-ARM (CONTROL ARM)
The triangulated link connecting the upright to the chassis, named for its A shape. Typically built from thin-wall steel or aluminum tube with rod ends at each pivot.
S.03
UPRIGHT (KNUCKLE)
The component that carries the wheel bearing/hub and connects to the upper and lower A-arms, tie rod, and (usually) the brake caliper mount. One of the highest-load, highest-consequence parts on the car.
S.04
CAMBER
The tilt of the wheel from vertical when viewed from the front — negative camber leans the top of the tire inward. Camber gain describes how this angle changes through suspension travel.
S.05
SCRUB RADIUS
The distance, viewed from the front, between the steering axis (kingpin) and the tire contact patch center at the ground. Affects steering feel and how braking forces feed back through the steering.
S.06
BUMP STEER
Unwanted toe change as the suspension moves through bump/droop travel, caused by the tie rod's arc not matching the A-arms' arc. A design defect to minimize, not a tuning tool.
S.07
ROLL CENTER
The instantaneous point about which the sprung mass rolls at a given axle, geometrically derived from the suspension linkage. Its height and how it migrates through roll strongly influence weight transfer and handling balance.
S.08
ROLL STIFFNESS (DISTRIBUTION)
Resistance to body roll under cornering load, split between front and rear axles via spring rate and anti-roll bar. The front/rear split is a primary handling-balance tuning lever.
S.09
MOTION RATIO
The ratio of damper travel to wheel travel at a given suspension position, used to translate a target wheel rate into the actual spring rate needed at the damper.
S.10
SPRING RATE
The force required to compress a spring a given distance, typically in lb/in or N/mm — a core input to ride frequency and roll stiffness.
S.11
ROCKER / BELLCRANK
A pivoting lever that transfers force from a pushrod or pullrod to the spring/damper, commonly used to package the damper out of the airflow and to tune motion ratio independent of A-arm geometry.
S.12
ROD END (HEIM JOINT)
A spherical bearing joint threaded onto the end of a link, allowing rotation in multiple axes so the link behaves as a true pin joint — matching the assumptions of kinematics software.
S.13
CORNER WEIGHTING
Adjusting ride height/spring perch at each corner so static load is distributed as intended across all four tires, done on scales before track testing.
S.14
ACKERMANN GEOMETRY
A steering linkage geometry where the inside wheel turns a sharper angle than the outside wheel during a turn, matching the different radii each wheel actually travels. See Steering.
S.15
WHEELBASE
The distance between front and rear axle centerlines. Shorter wheelbases generally turn in more eagerly — FSAE sets a 60 in floor.
S.16
TRACK
The distance between the centerlines of the left and right tires on the same axle. Wider track resists roll better at the cost of packaging room.
S.17
DAMPER (SHOCK)
A hydraulic device that resists spring oscillation by forcing oil through small metered holes in a piston — larger holes mean faster flow and less resistance. Often paired with a remote reservoir to prevent the fluid from overheating.
S.18
LINEAR VS. PROGRESSIVE SPRING
A linear (straight-wound) spring compresses at a constant rate throughout its travel. A progressive spring compresses quickly at first, then stiffens toward the end of its range — useful for handling a wide range of loads with one spring.
S.19
WEDGE
A car is "wedged" when the majority of its weight sits on a diagonal pair of wheels (e.g. front-left/rear-right). Caster and static setup both influence how much wedge the car carries into a corner.
S.20
CONTACT PATCH
The actual area of tire touching the road at any instant — the only thing transmitting grip to the car. Every alignment parameter (camber, caster, toe) exists to shape this patch under load.
SHEET 03 / STRUCTURES

Brakes

Converts kinetic energy to heat fast enough to stop the car within the rulebook's requirements — and does it through two fully independent circuits.
CIRCUITSDual independent (FSAE req.)
TOOLSHand calc + bench test
PRIMARY OUTPUTRotor/caliper/MC spec
KEY REVIEWTech inspection brake test
DESIGN PROCESS — BRAKES
01

Define the performance target and rules constraints

Pull the current rulebook's braking requirements: dual independent hydraulic circuits (so a single failure still stops two wheels), a functioning brake-over-travel switch, and the deceleration/lockup demonstration required at tech inspection. These are pass/fail gates before the car ever turns a lap.

02

Brake bias calculation

Compute weight transfer under maximum deceleration to find the theoretical ideal front/rear brake bias — the split of braking torque between axles. Because braking load moves weight forward, the front circuit typically does significantly more work than a static weight split would suggest.

03

Rotor sizing

Size rotor diameter and thickness for the brake torque needed at each axle and the thermal capacity to handle repeated stops without excessive fade, all while checking rotor OD clears the wheel and upright package from Suspension step 04.

04

Caliper and pad selection

Match caliper piston area and pad compound to the rotor and target clamping force. This is usually a selection exercise from available FSAE-scale hardware rather than a from-scratch design — the engineering work is in matching the system, not the individual part.

05

Master cylinder and pedal ratio

Size master cylinder bore and pedal ratio so the driver can generate enough line pressure at a reasonable pedal effort — too small a bore needs unreasonable pedal force, too large gives poor modulation and a long pedal travel.

06

Bias adjustment and circuit routing

Add a bias bar or proportioning valve so bias is driver- or setup-adjustable, then route the two circuits so a single line failure genuinely leaves two independent wheels braking, exactly as the rules require — not just on paper, but in physical routing.

07

Bleed, bed-in, and test

Bleed both circuits fully, bed in pads and rotors per the manufacturer's procedure, then run the full lockup/deceleration test the same way tech inspection will before you ever trust the system on track.

TERM
DEFINITION
B.01
BRAKE BIAS
The proportion of total braking torque delivered to the front vs. rear axle. Set too far rearward and the rear locks first, risking a spin; too far forward wastes rear grip.
B.02
WEIGHT TRANSFER
The dynamic shift of vertical load toward the front axle under braking (and rearward under acceleration), driven by deceleration rate and center of gravity height. Directly sets the ideal brake bias.
B.03
MASTER CYLINDER (MC)
Converts pedal force into hydraulic line pressure. FSAE cars run two — one per circuit — actuated by a common pedal through a balance/bias bar.
B.04
PEDAL RATIO
The mechanical advantage between the driver's foot and the master cylinder pushrod, set by pedal pivot geometry. A key lever for achievable line pressure at reasonable pedal effort.
B.05
BIAS BAR
A mechanism at the pedal that adjusts how pedal force splits between the front and rear master cylinders, letting brake bias be tuned without changing hardware.
B.06
CALIPER
The component that clamps brake pads onto the rotor using hydraulic pistons. Fixed calipers (pistons on both sides) are stiffer and more common in FSAE than floating designs.
B.07
BRAKE TORQUE
The resisting torque generated at the wheel by clamping force, pad friction coefficient, and effective rotor radius — the quantity that ultimately has to overcome the car's momentum.
B.08
THERMAL FADE
Loss of braking performance from overheated pads/rotors reducing the effective friction coefficient — a risk on repeated hard stops (endurance events especially) if rotor mass and cooling are undersized.
B.09
BOTS (Brake-Over-Travel Switch)
A rules-required switch that cuts power/ignition if the brake pedal travels beyond its normal range, indicating a hydraulic failure. A required safety system, checked at tech inspection.
B.10
BLEEDING
The process of purging air from a hydraulic brake circuit so the pedal transmits force through incompressible fluid rather than a compressible air pocket — a spongy pedal is almost always an air/bleed problem.
SHEET 04 / STRUCTURES

Steering

The link between driver input and front wheel angle — small in part count, high in consequence if geometry or the quick-release fails.
SYSTEMRack and pinion
COUPLED WITHSuspension geometry
PRIMARY OUTPUTRack, column, tie rods
KEY REVIEWBump steer test
DESIGN PROCESS — STEERING
01

Define steering ratio and Ackermann targets

Choose overall steering ratio (how much wheel angle you get per degree of steering wheel input) and how much Ackermann effect you want. Full Ackermann has the inside wheel turning a tighter angle than the outside wheel, which reduces scrub and needs less effort to steer through; reverse Ackermann runs the tires nearly parallel through a turn instead. FSAE cars often run reduced or "anti"-Ackermann relative to a road car, since low-speed parking maneuverability matters far less than tire behavior at racing slip angles.

02

Rack selection and mounting

Select or design a rack and pinion unit sized for your required travel and packaged so tie rod angles stay clean through the suspension's full range of motion, coordinating directly with the suspension hardpoints from step 04 of that process.

03

Column, quick-release, and driver ergonomics

Design the steering column with U-joints to route around the driver's legs, and include a quick-release hub per rules so the wheel can be removed for driver egress. Steering wheel size, shape, and lock-to-lock travel should be set with actual driver fit sessions, not assumed — target 1 to 1¼ turns lock-to-lock so the driver never has to shuffle their hands, and make sure your resulting turning radius is tighter than the tightest corner on course, with margin for understeer.

04

Tie rod and steering arm geometry

Position the tie rod inner and outer pickup points so its arc through suspension travel matches the A-arms' arc as closely as possible — this is the single biggest lever against bump steer, and it's solved jointly with the suspension kinematics model, not steering alone.

05

Structural verification

Run FEA or hand calcs on the column and steering arm against the rulebook's required steering system load case — this is a system the rules specifically expect to survive a driver leaning hard on the wheel, not just normal driving loads.

06

Assemble and validate

Install, then physically measure bump steer through the suspension's full travel and compare against the kinematics model. Small discrepancies here are normal; large ones usually mean a tie rod pickup point moved during fabrication or the rack mount isn't where CAD assumed. Always assemble and check on a genuinely level surface — check both planes with a level, not just by eye — since KPI is what gives the wheel its self-centering effect (forcing the tire down and the body up as it turns), and a false-level check will make correctly-built geometry look wrong.

The single biggest lever on lap time here isn't more caster, it's less load change on the inside wheel — a smaller, steadier angle beats a bigger, unstable one.

DEFINITION
T.01
STEERING RATIO
The ratio of steering wheel rotation to front wheel angle change. A lower (quicker) ratio gives sharper response but less mechanical advantage and more sensitivity to driver input error.
T.02
RACK AND PINION
A steering mechanism converting the rotation of a pinion gear into the linear motion of a rack, which pushes/pulls the tie rods to steer the front wheels.
T.03
TIE ROD
The link connecting the steering rack to the steering arm on the upright, translating rack motion into wheel angle. Its pickup points are the primary control over bump steer.
T.04
STEERING ARM
The lever arm off the upright that the tie rod pulls on to rotate the wheel about its steering axis. Its length and angle set the effective Ackermann behavior.
T.05
ACKERMANN GEOMETRY
A geometric relationship where the inside wheel steers a sharper angle than the outside wheel in a turn, matching their different turn radii. Full Ackermann matches this exactly and reduces scrub/steering effort; reverse Ackermann runs the tires nearly parallel instead. FSAE cars often intentionally run less than full.
T.06
STEERING COLUMN
The shaft (often in segments joined by U-joints) transmitting rotation from the steering wheel to the rack's pinion, routed around the driver's legs and cockpit structure.
T.07
U-JOINT (Universal Joint)
A pivoting coupling allowing a steering shaft to change angle across its length — necessary since the column rarely runs in a single straight line from wheel to rack.
T.08
LOCK-TO-LOCK
The total steering wheel rotation from full-left to full-right lock. FSAE cars typically target 1 to 1¼ turns, tight enough that the driver never shuffles their hands.
T.09
BUMP STEER
Unintended toe change as the suspension travels through bump/droop, caused by a mismatch between the tie rod's arc and the A-arms' arc. See also Suspension glossary.
T.10
KING PIN INCLINATION (KPI)
The inward tilt of the steering pivot axis, viewed from the front. Gives the wheel a self-centering effect in a turn — as the wheel turns, KPI forces the tire down and the body up, pulling the wheel back toward straight-ahead.
T.11
CASTER
The tilt of the steering axis viewed from the side — the angle between the steering axis and the car's vertical axis. Adds straight-line stability and loads the inside wheel in a turn; faster cars generally want more of it.
T.12
TOE
How much the front (or rear) wheels point in or out from straight-ahead, viewed from above. Toe-in adds stability; toe-out adds turn-in response at the cost of stability — most cars want a small amount of toe-in to start.
T.13
TURNING RADIUS
The tightest circle the car can turn within at full lock. Needs to beat the tightest corner on any course you'll run, with margin for understeer eating into it.

Team Practices

How we work matters as much as what we build. These habits apply to every subteam, not just structures — read them as seriously as the technical sheets above.

DESIGN PRIORITIESIN ROUGH ORDER
SAFETY & RELIABILITYNon-negotiableThe single most important part of the design — full stop, before any of the below.
WEIGHT DISTRIBUTIONCentralize massBattery, harness, and heavy components pull weight rearward — actively work to bring mass toward the car's center.
POWER-TO-WEIGHT & SHAPEOptimize togetherOutput and efficiency come from body shape and mass working together, not either alone.
TEST & ITERATE1–2 year cycle idealIf compressed to one year, build as simple a car as possible and protect time for adjustment — don't spend it all on the build.
DO
  • Make every second count — always be doing something.
  • Keep everything organized: parts, notes, and ideas should be findable by anyone.
  • Design and build subsystems in parallel, but talk constantly so integration isn't a nightmare at the end.
  • Pick the design that can actually be built in time, not just the best one on paper.
  • Do your hardest thinking when you're rested and clear-headed, not the night before a deadline.
  • Cross-train — anyone should be able to jump onto a different system on short notice.
  • Ask other teams, advisors, and mentors for help. Nobody loses points for asking.
  • Cut your largest stock pieces first, and measure twice before every cut.
  • Treat the rulebook and service manuals as your best friends.
  • Have fun — it's most of why we're here.
AVOID
  • Standing around without a clear assignment.
  • Ordering parts before you're sure they'll actually integrate.
  • Betting the schedule on a delivery date with no backup plan.
  • Treating the rulebook as an enemy instead of a spec.
  • Working fatigued — tired hands make expensive mistakes.
  • Assuming you can finish a build at competition.
  • Letting personal issues on the team go unaddressed.
  • Reaching for extreme, unproven designs.
  • Making a design choice before you've run the numbers.
